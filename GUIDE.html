Start here

Three things worth doing in the first five minutes.

See the twelve best sites right now

  1. Open index.html.
  2. Click ★ Top picks.
  3. Each card explains in one line why it ranks where it does. Click any card for the HR contact and phone number.

Find everything close enough to service on a lunch break

  1. In the sidebar under Distance from home, click bands 2 3 4 to switch them off. Only band 1 stays on.
  2. Go to ☰ Ranked list. You now have the 56 sites inside five miles, ranked best first.
  3. Star the ones worth a call, then flip on Only my shortlist to work through them.

Build a call list you can actually work from

  1. Filter and sort until the list looks right.
  2. Click ↓ Export CSV. You get the visible rows, in the visible order, with HR name, phone, website and every score.
  3. Open it in Excel and start dialling.

04

Search

Top of the sidebar. Press / from anywhere to jump into it.

One box, matched against seven fields at once: company name, street address, city, ZIP, the NAICS business description, the industry label the app assigned, and the HR contact name. Matching is case-insensitive and partial, so nevada finds every site on N Nevada Ave and hospital finds both the venues named "Hospital" and the ones whose NAICS description mentions one.

Search stacks with every other filter rather than replacing it — if bands 3 and 4 are switched off, searching only looks inside the remaining sites. Clear it with the ✕ on the right of the box.

05

Sort by

Up to four levels, applied in the order they are listed.

How a rule works

Numbered badge
The level. Level 1 decides the order; level 2 only breaks ties in level 1, and so on. So Industry ↑ then Placement score ↓ groups the list by industry and ranks within each group.
Dropdown
Picks the field. Changing it resets that level to the field's natural direction — distance defaults to nearest-first, score to highest-first.
↓ / ↑
Flips the direction of that level. ↓ is largest or latest first; ↑ is smallest or A–Z first.
↑ (second arrow)
Promotes the rule one level up, so you can reorder priority without deleting and re-adding.
✕
Removes the level. Remove them all and the app falls back to placement score, highest first.
+ Add level
Adds another rule, pre-filled with the first field you are not already sorting on. Greys out at four.

The grey line underneath — Applied in order: … — always spells out the sort in plain words, so you can confirm the list is doing what you think it is.

06

Score weights

Five sliders. They decide what "best" means, and everything downstream follows.

Each site carries five sub-scores, all on a 0–100 scale. The placement score is their weighted mean. The sliders set the weights; the percentages shown always add to 100 no matter where you drag them, so you are setting proportions, not absolute values. Move one and the ranked list, the top picks, the map ordering and every chart recompute immediately.

Audience size30%
How many people are at that address on a normal day — corrected staff plus visitors. Log-scaled, so the gap between 50 and 500 people counts for far more than the gap between 2,500 and 3,000.
Proximity25%
How cheap and quick the site is to restock. Decays smoothly with driving distance rather than stepping at the band edges: 100 at the door, about 64 at five miles, 41 at ten, 17 at twenty.
Industry fit25%
How well vending converts at that kind of venue. A fixed value per venue type — 95 for a hospital or a factory floor, 35 for a restaurant that sells food itself. Full table in §8.
Captive audience12%
How easily people can leave for food instead. High on a secure campus or a hospital night shift; low at a downtown office ringed by lunch spots.
Route density8%
How many other listed sites sit within 1.5 miles. A cluster means one restock trip can serve several machines, which changes the economics of the whole route rather than one placement.
Reset
Puts all five back to 30 / 25 / 25 / 12 / 8.
Worth trying

Drag Proximity to zero. The list becomes "best sites in the county, ignore the drive" — useful for deciding whether a distant hospital is worth the trip. Then drag it to the top and everything else down: that is the "which machine can I top up on the way home" list.

Read the map carefully here

The dashed rings drawn on the map are a straight-line radius. The band colours follow real driving distance. A site can sit just inside the 5-mile ring and still be coloured yellow, because the road there loops around a canyon or crosses I-25 twice. The colour is the honest number; the ring is only a visual anchor.

Industry type

Twenty venue types. Click any row to include or exclude it; All and None are in the panel header.

Each company was placed into a venue type from its NAICS code, its business description and its name. The venue type is not just a label — it carries four numbers that shape everything the app says about that site.

  • Fit — how well vending converts there, 0–100. Feeds the Industry fit sub-score.
  • Dwell — how captive the audience is, 0–100. Feeds the Captive audience sub-score.
  • On site — the share of reported headcount actually at that address on a given day.
  • Visitors — daily non-employee footfall, as a multiple of the on-site staff. A hotel at 2.2× sees more than three times as many guests as staff.
  • 7-day — whether the site runs weekends and multiple shifts. Sets the month at 30 operating days instead of 21.

Why the on-site column matters more than it looks

Master Klean Janitorial reports 650 employees. At 15% on site, the app credits it with about 98 people — the rest are in other people's buildings. A 250-person hospital at 75% plus a 0.9× visitor flow is credited with roughly 356. That single correction is why the ranking does not simply follow headcount, and it is the main reason the list is worth more than sorting the spreadsheet in Excel.

The map letter is what appears inside each pin when you switch the map to colour by industry — so the map stays readable if two venue colours are hard to tell apart.

Refine

Four ranges and two switches, at the bottom of the sidebar.

Min employees
Hides anything below the figure. Runs from 50 (the smallest in your list) to 3,162. Push it to 200 or so to strip out the small offices.
Max employees
Hides anything above it. Useful in reverse — capping at 300 shows the mid-size sites that a national vending operator is less likely to have already signed. The two sliders push each other rather than crossing over.
Max drive time
Caps one-way driving minutes. Opens at 65, which includes everything. Drop it to 15 for the sites you can reach inside a quarter of an hour.
Min placement score
Hides anything scoring below the value. Because the score depends on your weight sliders, this filter moves when you change the weights — it is a quality floor, not a fixed cut.
Only venues where staff are on site
Hides the three venue types whose workforce is mostly somewhere else: Construction & Trades (25% on site), Residential & Apartments (30%) and Field & Facility Services (15%). Fifty sites in all. Turn it on when you want a call list with no wasted visits.
Only my shortlist
Narrows every tab — the map included — to the sites you starred. For working through the shortlist with contacts and totals, use the Shortlist tab instead.

Record confidence

Every site was cross-checked. The sidebar panel filters on the result; the badge appears everywhere the company does.

The check answers "does this record hold together" — not "is this business solvent". No public source can tell you the latter, and the app does not pretend otherwise. What it can do is tell you which records are independently confirmed and which have something worth two minutes of your time before you drive out.

The four tiers

BadgeMeansSites
✓ VerifiedA business of this name sits at this location on OpenStreetMap, and nothing else looks off.59
• ConsistentNothing contradicts the record — but nothing independently confirms it either. Most of the list.268
! One thing to checkOne check failed. The badge shows the count; the detail panel says which.38
!! Needs a checkTwo or more failed. None in the current list.0

Where the evidence comes from

External. All 365 addresses were matched against an OpenStreetMap extract of El Paso and Teller counties — 9,486 named places pulled through the Overpass API. Where a business of the same name sits within 400 m of the geocoded point, the record is Verified. Where the street address exists but under a different business name, that is recorded too, since it usually means the tenant changed.

The important caveat

A missing OpenStreetMap record proves nothing. Most ordinary offices are simply not mapped — 259 of the 365 have no OSM entry at all. Absence never raises a warning on its own, and you should not read "Consistent" as suspicion.

Internal. Seven deterministic checks run over the spreadsheet itself:

CheckWhat it catchesLevelHits
Phone area codeNumber rings a corporate switchboard outside Colorado, so the "HR Director" is not the person who decides what goes in that break room.warning33
Approximate locationThe address fell back to a ZIP centroid or a named-place match, so the pin may be off by a block or more.warning4
No street numberThe address is a mail code or PO box, not a building you can walk into.warning1
Shared addressOther listed companies occupy the same building — the headcount may belong to the whole tower, so treat the audience as an upper bound.note43
Repeat companyThe same company appears more than once, at different sites. Don't count it twice.note52
Website mismatchThe domain does not resemble the company name — often a parent or an acquirer.note130
Shared phoneTwo companies listed with the same number, a common list-broker artefact.note0

Only the first three raise a warning and affect the tier. The rest are context shown in the detail panel. Your list has no duplicate phone numbers at all, which is a good sign about where it came from.

Settling a flag

Open the site's detail panel. The Record confidence section lists every check with its result, and two buttons underneath open a web search for that exact company and address, and a Google Maps view of the building. Ten seconds usually settles it.

The one to act on

Of the 38 flagged records, 33 are out-of-state phone numbers. That is the single most useful thing the check found: for those sites, the number in your sheet reaches a national HR line, not the local facility. Call the site's main number instead, or ask the switchboard for the local facilities manager.

Map

Where the sites actually are, relative to your door.

Reading a pin

Three things are encoded in every marker at once. Colour is the distance band, the numeral inside repeats that band so the map still reads if the colours are hard to tell apart, and size scales with estimated daily audience — the biggest circles are the sites with the most people in the building. Hovering lifts a pin above its neighbours; the highest-scoring pins already sit on top where markers overlap.

Hover, then click

Hover a pin for a one-line tooltip: company, drive distance and time, daily audience, score, and a warning line if the record has flags. Good for skimming a cluster without committing.

Click and the full detail panel opens straight away — no intermediate popup, no second click. Click the next pin and the panel swaps to it, so you can work through a neighbourhood one pin at a time. The map only recentres when the pin you clicked is off screen, so the view stays where you put it.

Pins with record flags carry an orange ring. Everything in the panel is described in §17.

If the map is blank

The map tiles are the one thing the app fetches from the internet. Everything else — ranking, filtering, the analytics charts, export — works with no connection. A blank grey map means the tiles could not load, not that anything is broken.

18

Changing your home base

Click the ★ home base chip in the top bar. Ten seconds, no command line.

Every distance, drive time, proximity score, servicing cost and net figure in the app is measured from one address. Type a new one and all 365 sites are re-routed on the real road network — the ranking, the map rings, the bands and the economics all move with it.

What happens when you press Set home base

  1. The address goes to the US Census geocoder, falling back to OpenStreetMap if the Census cannot place it.
  2. All 365 sites are re-routed through OSRM in batches, with a progress bar.
  3. Driving miles, drive times, straight-line distances, distance bands, proximity scores, servicing costs and net contributions are recomputed.
  4. The map recentres, the rings move, and every tab updates.

The log tells you exactly what happened at each step. If routing cannot be reached, distances fall back to straight-line with a road factor applied and the log says so plainly — usually within 10–15% of the real drive, but set the address again once you are online for exact figures.

Different people, different home bases

Your home base is stored in your browser, alongside the routed distances so it is only computed once. Someone else opening the same file on their own machine gets their own home base and their own rankings, without touching yours. Reset to original puts it back to 3544 Cape Romain Dr instantly, with no network needed.

Two ways to change it

The button is the fast way and is per-person. The command line — python3 scripts/pipeline.py "data/List.xlsx" --home "…" then python3 scripts/build.py — bakes a new default into index.html for everyone who opens it. Use the button for yourself; use the pipeline when you want to hand the file to someone with a different origin already set.

19

Import a spreadsheet

Two ways in. The browser is easier; the command line is faster and matches more addresses.

In the browser

Click ↑ Import Excel, then drop an .xlsx, .xls or .csv onto the dashed box, or click it to browse. Columns are matched by name — Company Name, Address, City, State, ZIP, Phone, Website, Number of Employees, a description column and First Name / Last Name are all found automatically. Only a company name and an address are strictly required; everything else degrades gracefully.

The log tells you what it found before you commit. Then click Geocode & load and it runs three passes:

  1. Every address to the US Census geocoder, one at a time, with a progress bar.
  2. Anything the Census could not match retried against OpenStreetMap, throttled to one a second.
  3. Every located site routed through OSRM in batches for real driving distance and time. Anything routing misses falls back to straight-line distance with a road factor applied.

A few hundred rows takes a couple of minutes. Geocodes are cached in the browser, so re-importing the same list is nearly instant. Rows that cannot be located at all are dropped and reported.

An import is not saved

It lives in the page until you reload. To keep it, click { } to download the enriched dataset, save it over data/companies.json, then run python3 scripts/build.py in the project folder. That bakes it into index.html as the new default.

From the command line

Faster, and it uses the Census batch endpoint, which matches more addresses than the one-at-a-time browser call:

pip3 install openpyxl requests python3 scripts/pipeline.py "data/Your New List.xlsx" python3 scripts/build.py

This is also the only way to change the home base, since it is baked into the data:

python3 scripts/pipeline.py "data/Your List.xlsx" \ --home "123 Main St, Colorado Springs, CO" python3 scripts/build.py

Then reopen index.html. The pipeline prints its progress and tells you how many addresses it located and routed.

What the app remembers

Between sessions, in your browser: your theme, your score weights, your sort rules, your shortlist, and the geocode cache from any import. Filters are deliberately not remembered — the app opens showing everything, so you never start from a filtered view you forgot you set.

All of this lives in the browser you opened the file with. Open the same file in a different browser and you get a fresh shortlist.

The arithmetic

Every number the app shows, in full.

Audience

on site = employees × on-site share audience = on site × (1 + visitor multiplier)

The five sub-scores

audience score = log₁₀(audience) rescaled to 0–100 (≈13 people → 0, ≈2,500 → 100) proximity score = 100 × e^(−miles ÷ 11) (0 mi → 100, 5 → 64, 10 → 41, 20 → 17) industry fit = the venue type's fit value captive = the venue type's dwell value route density = log(neighbours within 1.5 mi) rescaled to 0–100

Placement score

score = Σ(sub-score × weight) ÷ Σ(weights)

Weights are normalised, so the score always lands on 0–100 whatever you set the sliders to.

Economics

capture rate = 5% + (industry fit ÷ 100) × 15% → 5%–20% of the audience vends daily days = 30 if the site runs 7 days, else 21 revenue = audience × capture rate × $2.75 × days gross = revenue × 52% servicing = 2 × ( miles × 2 × $0.70 + (minutes × 2 + 15) ÷ 60 × $28 ) net = gross − servicing

Servicing assumes two restock trips a month, a round trip each, mileage at $0.70 and time at $28 an hour, plus fifteen minutes at the machine.

What these numbers are for

They rank sites against each other honestly and consistently. They are not a quote and not a business plan. What you actually earn depends on the commission you agree, what is already in the building, and how the product mix lands — none of which is in your spreadsheet.

How do I…

…find the closest sites that are actually worth it?

  1. Leave only band 1 on.
  2. Turn on Only venues where staff are on site.
  3. Drag Min placement score up until roughly fifteen rows remain.

…find sites where one trip could serve several machines?

  1. Set sort level 1 to Route density (1.5 mi).
  2. Add level 2: Placement score ↓.
  3. Open the top result and read Other listed sites ≤ 1.5 mi. Then search that street name to see who the neighbours are.

…work only the venue types that convert best?

  1. Click None in the Industry panel.
  2. Switch on Healthcare, Manufacturing, Logistics, Aerospace and Call Center — the five types rated 90 or above.
  3. Sort by Daily on-site audience ↓.

…compare two specific companies?

  1. Search one, open its detail panel, click Copy details.
  2. Paste it somewhere.
  3. Repeat for the other. The two blocks line up field for field.

…see what the ranking looks like if I stop caring about the drive?

  1. Drag Proximity to 0.
  2. Go to Top picks. Compare against what was there before.
  3. Reset puts the weights back.

…produce a call list for tomorrow morning?

  1. Filter to what you want to work, and star each site worth a call.
  2. Open the Shortlist tab and sort by Drive time ↑ so it runs in route order.
  3. Check the Servicing all of them tile — if that is more hours than you have, star fewer.
  4. Export call sheet. Contacts and phone numbers are in it.

…work from a different starting address?

  1. Click the ★ home base chip in the top bar.
  2. Type the address with city and state, press Set home base, wait about ten seconds.
  3. Everything re-measures. Reset to original undoes it instantly.

…avoid records with something wrong with them?

  1. In Record confidence, switch off One thing to check and Needs a check.
  2. Or leave them on and sort by Record warnings ↓ to deal with the questionable ones first and clear them.

…check whether a pin is where I think it is?

  1. Open the site's detail panel.
  2. Read Geocode quality. Anything other than Exact rooftop match deserves a look.
  3. Click Directions ↗ and confirm against Google's own result.

…find the biggest employers regardless of everything else?

  1. Sort level 1: Employees (total) ↓.
  2. Compare the Employees column against Audience. Where they diverge sharply, the venue type is telling you the headcount is not in that building.

When it looks wrong

The map is grey and empty
Map tiles need an internet connection. Everything else works without one. If you are online and it is still grey, switch to another tab and back — the map re-measures itself when the tab is shown.
"No sites match these filters"
Something in the sidebar is too tight. The usual suspects are Min placement score and the Industry panel with most types switched off. Click All on the bands and the industries to get back to a clean slate.
A company is in the wrong industry
Open its detail panel and read Classified via. Classification uses the company name first, then the NAICS description, then the numeric code — so a firm whose sheet description is Office Administrative Services can land somewhere generic. The classification rules live in scripts/pipeline.py and are easy to add to.
A pin is in the wrong place
Check Geocode quality. Two sites in the current dataset fell back to a ZIP-code centroid and sit at the middle of their postal area rather than at the building.
An import produced fewer rows than my file has
Rows with no company name or no address are skipped before geocoding, and rows that could not be located anywhere are dropped afterwards. The log in the import dialog reports both counts.
Setting a home base says the address was not found
Include the city and state — 1234 Example St, Colorado Springs, CO. The US Census geocoder wants a real street address; a business name alone will not resolve. A ZIP helps but is not required.
Distances look off after changing the home base
Read the log in the dialog. If it says routing could not be reached, every distance is a straight-line estimate with a road factor applied. Set the address again once you are online.
A company is flagged but looks fine to me
Most flags are context, not accusations — the commonest by far is an out-of-state phone number, which just means the contact is a corporate line. Open the panel and read the actual flag before discounting a site.
My shortlist disappeared
It is stored per browser. Opening the file in a different browser, or in a private window, starts an empty one.
I want to start completely fresh
Reload the page. Filters reset on their own; click Reset under Score weights and All on both filter panels to clear the rest.
